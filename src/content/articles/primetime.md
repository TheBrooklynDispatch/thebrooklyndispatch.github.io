*Primetime* is most interesting when it asks a question it never quite manages to answer: how much of ourselves are we willing to lose in pursuit of the thing we have decided will make us whole?

The film follows that question into increasingly ugly territory. Ambition becomes fixation, professional instinct becomes moral compromise, and the distance between observing something and participating in it begins to collapse. What works is that the film never seems especially interested in excusing any of this. It is not arguing that obsession justifies cruelty, or that professional success somehow cleanses bad behavior. Instead, it is interested in the quieter and more uncomfortable process by which bad behavior gets absorbed into a larger goal. The compromises accumulate. Each one becomes easier to rationalize because there is always something further ahead worth reaching.

That inevitably calls to mind *Nightcrawler* (2014), another film about a person discovering that the machinery of media rewards precisely the instincts that should make us uncomfortable. But where Dan Gilroy’s film steadily tightens its moral logic until Lou Bloom and the system around him become almost indistinguishable, *Primetime* is messier. That is sometimes to its benefit. Robert Pattinson’s character is not quite the perfectly engineered sociopath of *Nightcrawler*. He is recognizably a person who has allowed the pursuit of his work to overwhelm his sense of proportion: the familiar “not especially good man consumed by his profession” archetype, but played with enough instability that Pattinson keeps him from becoming entirely predictable.

Pattinson will get most of the attention for the performance, and rightly so. He gives the film much of its nervous momentum. But the other central performance is just as important, in part because the character is less conventional. His storyline never fits as cleanly into the idea of a talented man slowly consumed by his own ambition. That makes him harder to reduce to a type, and at times more compelling. The contrast between the two men hints at a richer movie about the different ways people disappear into the lives they are trying to build.

The problem is that *Primetime* generates more interpretive possibilities than its storytelling can comfortably hold. Some of the wide range of reactions to the film will come from its genuine ambiguity, which I think is a strength. Audiences do not need to be told exactly how to feel about every character or moral choice. But some of that ambiguity also seems to come from the movie’s pacing, particularly in its final stretch. The climax should be where its competing ideas finally acquire weight. Instead, the stakes begin to dissipate.

There is an important difference between leaving an audience unsettled and leaving them hollow. I am happy for a film to deny me a clean moral conclusion. In fact, *Primetime* would probably be worse if it neatly explained what its characters had become or told us how to judge them. But the ending does not simply leave those questions open; it leaves too much of the emotional architecture unfinished. The movie reaches its climax without fully convincing us that everything preceding it has converged there.

That weakness also exposes some of the film’s less integrated material. The India subplot, in particular, feels like an idea that should have remained in the writers’ room. It gestures toward another dimension of the film without becoming essential to the characters, themes, or momentum around it. Rather than complicating the story, it mostly makes the film feel less disciplined.

And yet there is something difficult to shake about *Primetime*. Its central question is a good one because it is not confined to journalism, media, or even explicitly immoral professions. Most ambition asks for some degree of self-invention. We decide what we want to become, then begin reorganizing our lives around that imagined person. The more interesting question is when that process stops being constructive and becomes corrosive: when achieving the thing starts requiring the abandonment of whatever made it worth wanting in the first place.

*Primetime* understands that danger better than it dramatizes it. Its ideas linger longer than its climax does. That may be why the film invites so much interpretation. There is clearly something substantial inside it, even when the movie itself never quite finds the shape capable of containing it.
