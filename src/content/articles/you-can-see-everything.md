![You Can See Everything](/assets/images/articles/holmes.jpg)

There is a peculiar confidence in the belief that, if someone could only see enough of our lives, they would eventually understand us. It is a confidence Elizabeth Holmes seems to possess in abundance. In *You Can See Everything*, Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim's remarkable documentary, the disgraced Theranos founder allows cameras into her life in the period before she enters prison. The apparent proposition is simple enough: look closely, and perhaps the person behind the headlines will become visible.

What makes the film extraordinary is how thoroughly it undermines that proposition.

I went into *You Can See Everything* with a reasonably settled understanding of Elizabeth Holmes. Most of us probably did. Her story has been told often enough that the broad outlines have hardened into something approaching cultural shorthand: the black turtleneck, the cultivated voice, the extraordinary promises, the spectacular collapse. She has become one of those public figures whose image seems to have overtaken the person herself. There is a familiar narrative, and we know where it ends.

But something strange happens when you spend this much time watching her. Your perception begins to shift. A moment of apparent sincerity makes you reconsider an earlier suspicion. An uncomfortable exchange restores it. You find yourself sympathizing with someone you had little expectation of sympathizing with, then questioning the very impulse that brought you there. By the end, I felt less certain about Holmes than I had at the beginning.

That uncertainty is not a failure of the documentary. It is the film's great achievement.

Documentaries have long operated on an implicit promise of access. The camera takes us somewhere we could not otherwise go, introduces us to people we could not otherwise meet, and gives us the feeling that we have witnessed something unmediated. Of course, we know intellectually that this is never quite true. Every image has a frame. Every conversation takes place in the presence of a camera. Every edit is a decision about what we are allowed to see, and in what order.

Still, the feeling persists. We watch people speak, hesitate, laugh, or cry, and some part of us believes we are getting closer to the truth.

*You Can See Everything* is unusually interested in the distance between that feeling and actual knowledge. Its title sounds almost like a promise, but gradually becomes a provocation. What does it mean to see everything? And why do we assume that seeing more necessarily means understanding more?

Holmes is an especially fascinating subject for these questions because her public identity was built around the manipulation of perception. Theranos was not merely a company selling a technological promise. It was also a story, carefully presented and repeated, about innovation, certainty, and the person who supposedly embodied both. Holmes understood the persuasive power of an image long before she became the subject of a documentary.

The temptation, then, is to watch her every gesture as evidence. A pause might suggest calculation. A smile might seem rehearsed. A moment of vulnerability might be genuine, or it might be another attempt to control how she is perceived. The problem is that none of these interpretations can be proven simply by looking.

And that is where the film becomes more unsettling than a conventional portrait of a disgraced public figure. It draws us into the act of interpretation, then makes us conscious of how unstable that act can be.

There are moments when Holmes seems recognizably human in ways that the public version of her rarely allows. The presence of family, the ordinary rhythms of domestic life, and the approaching reality of imprisonment complicate the easy distance between viewer and subject. Whatever we think of her actions, it is difficult to observe these circumstances without registering the emotional consequences for the people involved.

But compassion is not exoneration. Feeling something for a person does not require revising the facts of what they have done. One of the film's most interesting qualities is its refusal to treat those responses as mutually exclusive. Holmes can appear vulnerable without becoming innocent. She can be affectionate without becoming trustworthy. She can believe in her own account of events without that account becoming true.

The distinction matters because we tend to prefer people who can be understood in singular terms. We want a coherent explanation that will reconcile every contradiction. If someone has deceived others, we begin looking for evidence that every other part of their life is equally false. If we encounter something apparently genuine, we may feel an equally strong temptation to soften our judgment.

The film resists both impulses. It leaves room for the possibility that sincerity and deception can exist in the same person, sometimes so closely intertwined that even the person herself may not experience them as separate.

Fielder's involvement adds another layer to this uncertainty. His work has repeatedly explored the strange things that happen when people become conscious of being observed, and the equally strange things that happen when a filmmaker attempts to manufacture circumstances in which something authentic might emerge. His presence makes the camera feel less like an invisible witness than an active participant in whatever unfolds.

That is an important distinction. Holmes is not the only person shaping a narrative here. The filmmakers are shaping one too. Their choices determine what becomes visible, what remains outside the frame, and how we understand the relationship between one moment and the next.

Rather than pretending to stand outside the problem of manipulation, *You Can See Everything* seems fascinated by its own implication in it. A documentary can expose the construction of a public image while constructing another image in its place. It can challenge a subject's version of events without offering an entirely transparent version of its own.

There is something of Orson Welles's *F for Fake* (1973) in this preoccupation with performance and authenticity. Welles understood that the pleasures of being deceived are not entirely separate from the pleasures of watching a film. We know images can mislead us, yet we continue to surrender ourselves to them. The question is not simply whether the person on screen is telling the truth, but why we are so eager to believe that the screen might settle the matter.

What impressed me most about *You Can See Everything* was the way it made that question feel personal. My changing opinion of Holmes became part of the experience of watching the film. I would catch myself arriving at a conclusion, only to realize how little evidence I actually had for it. Then I would find myself doing it again.

This is a rare sensation in a documentary about someone so thoroughly covered by the media. Ordinarily, familiarity makes us feel more confident. Here, familiarity becomes another obstacle. We bring years of headlines, interviews, photographs, and dramatizations into the theater, along with the assumption that they have given us a reasonably complete picture. The film does not ask us to forget that history. It asks us to consider how much of our certainty has been built from images and narratives that were never as complete as they seemed.

None of this means the truth is unknowable, or that every account of Holmes deserves equal consideration. The facts of the Theranos case do not dissolve because a documentary complicates our feelings about its central figure. There is a difference between uncertainty about someone's interior life and uncertainty about documented events. The film is most compelling when it keeps that distinction intact.

What remains difficult to establish is not what happened, but who Holmes is beyond what happened. That may sound like a modest distinction, but it is one that much of our culture has become increasingly uncomfortable making. Public figures are reduced to stories we can recognize instantly. Their most revealing moments become clips, their contradictions become evidence, and our judgments begin to feel like knowledge.

*You Can See Everything* unsettles that confidence. It gives us more of Elizabeth Holmes than we might have expected to see, but refuses to make that proximity synonymous with understanding. The closer we get, the more conscious we become of the distance that remains.

I left the film having changed my mind about her several times. I cannot say that I arrived at a better or more forgiving judgment. What changed was my confidence in the idea that I could arrive at a complete one.

For a film ostensibly about a woman who became famous for persuading people to believe what they could not verify, that feels like an unusually profound place to end up. The camera can show us an extraordinary amount. It cannot guarantee that we know what we are looking at.
