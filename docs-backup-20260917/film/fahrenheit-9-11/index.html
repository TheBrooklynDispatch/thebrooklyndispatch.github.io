Fahrenheit 9/11: When a Documentary Already Knows the Answer

I think the best documentaries end up going places they don't expect, whereas Fahrenheit 9/11 feels methodically calculated from beginning to end

By Steve Berkowitz August 22, 2026

Watching Fahrenheit 9/11 twenty-two years later is a strange experience. It is an odd time capsule into a version of American life that now feels almost quaint, while simultaneously feeling dystopian in its reflection of our current political moment: vacation-taking presidents, wars pushed by corporate interests, contested elections, nonsensical politicians with dubious cross-border links, and increasingly polarized points of view. Michael Moore presents an America that feels like it is approaching some sort of political breaking point. From 2026, you watch knowing that it wasn't.

There is plenty in Fahrenheit 9/11 that still works, particularly Moore's sardonic humor. Britney Spears appears on television explaining that Americans should support the president. Ricky Martin pops up in a sequence about Bush's vacations, unable to get his flight into Texas because the president is there. Moore is very good at finding the absurdity in political imagery, and even better at editing that imagery so the joke barely needs explaining. But watching these sequences now, I kept thinking less about the political documentaries that followed Fahrenheit 9/11 and more about YouTube. The combination of news footage, archival clips, ironic juxtaposition and a narrator guiding us toward the contradiction has become a familiar way of consuming politics online. In that sense, Fahrenheit 9/11 feels strangely modern.

The problem is that Moore's POV eventually becomes the takeaway of the film rather than simply the means through which he examines his subject. I think the best documentaries end up going places they don't expect; Fahrenheit 9/11 feels methodically calculated from beginning to end. There is little sense that Moore might encounter something that complicates his argument, or even something he isn't quite sure what to do with. Every Bush stumble has a purpose. Every cutaway has an implication. Every piece of music tells you a little more about how to read the image underneath it. Moore's one-sidedness isn't exactly hidden — this is very obviously a hit piece on George W. Bush — but the certainty becomes more interesting when the evidence itself feels less certain than the voice presenting it.

The sequence of Bush sitting in a Florida elementary school on the morning of September 11 is where this bothered me most. After Andrew Card tells him that a second plane has struck the World Trade Center and that America is under attack, Bush stays in his chair for several minutes while the children continue reading. Moore lingers on him, wondering through voiceover what the president might possibly be thinking about: his administration's connections to Saudi Arabia, the bin Laden family, perhaps nothing at all. The longer Bush remains seated, the more absurd and incriminating Moore wants the footage to become. I found myself having almost the opposite reaction. I'm not sure what I would expect any human being to do in those first few minutes after quietly being told that their country is under attack, while sitting in an elementary school classroom surrounded by children and cameras. Most if not all of us would probably be paralyzed. Wanting to take a few minutes and be calculated rather than immediately reactionary doesn't strike me as an obvious failure of leadership.

That moment gets at my frustration with Moore's method because the footage itself is fascinating. Bush really did sit there. The expression on his face is strange to watch when you know what he has just been told. Moore doesn't need to invent anything. But rather than allowing that strangeness to sit for a moment, he immediately starts closing down what it might mean. Bush isn't processing something incomprehensible; he's incompetent. The silence isn't ambiguous; it's incriminating. It's moments like this where Moore's voiceover becomes less useful to me, because I'm more interested in the image than I am in his explanation of it. A documentary can show us exactly what happened and still have no idea what was happening inside someone's head. Moore rarely seems interested in that distinction.

None of which means there isn't compelling material here. Moore's examination of the Bush administration's relationships with Saudi interests, the selling of the Iraq War and, later, the young Americans actually asked to fight it contains plenty worth sitting with. His anger gives the film energy, and his humor makes political material that could easily become dry surprisingly entertaining. But the more convincing the film wants to be, the more I wanted Moore to occasionally let an image escape him. When every clip arrives with its meaning already attached, watching Fahrenheit 9/11 can start to feel less like watching an investigation than watching an extremely talented filmmaker assemble evidence for a case he decided long ago.

There is also a strange detail that Moore could never have anticipated. Before a documentary filled with political righteousness begins exposing the hidden relationships, compromises and hypocrisies of powerful people, a Weinstein production card appears on screen. It would be unfair to turn that into some retrospective gotcha against Moore; he couldn't know in 2004 what that name would come to mean. But seeing it now does add an uncomfortable wrinkle to a film so confident in identifying its villains. Twenty-two years is a long time for moral certainty to survive intact.

That certainty is what I kept returning to after Fahrenheit 9/11 ended. Moore makes several troubling and compelling points, and he's frequently very funny making them. But I was most interested in the moments where I could see something different in the footage than he could. Bush sitting in that classroom for several minutes is a strange, uncomfortable image. Maybe the problem isn't that Moore has an answer for it. It's that he never seems interested in the possibility of another one.
